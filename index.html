Beliefs

What I keep coming back to

A few things I believe about players and data, mostly learned the slow way at the field. Each one comes from something I wrote or built, so you can go check it.

  1. Players are people first and baseball players second.

    Two players can get the exact same information and respond to it completely differently, because they have different experiences, goals, and ways of understanding the game. The work is learning who someone is, then figuring out how to say the thing so he can actually use it.

    From my story, below

  2. Compare a player to himself and to his own goals.

    Rankings tell you where someone sits, but they don't tell you whether he is getting closer to the thing he is actually working on. I would rather know what a player's goals are and measure against those, especially when the results haven't caught up yet.

    How I write the weekly development reports at Columbia

  3. Judge a player on a number that hasn't stabilized and you're reading the sample, not the player.

    A college player gets a few months, which is way less time than a pro and with more at stake, so it really matters which numbers you lean on early. Whiff rate is trustworthy after about 59 plate appearances. OPS still isn't after a full season.

    Stabilization thread, Paradigm, Sep 2026

  4. Luck runs out. Skill is repeatable and trainable.

    OPS tells you who produced, but it can't tell a blooper that fell in from a line drive hit right at the shortstop. Before you change anything about a hitter, check whether the problem shows up in past seasons and in BP, because if it does, it's real and you can train it.

    Luck or Skill thread, Paradigm, Oct 2026

  5. A lot of what holds people back is that they just don't know how yet.

    Most of what I've built started because somebody, often me, didn't have a way to learn something before it counted. New taggers didn't have a place to practice, so I made one. Our pitchers couldn't easily find their own bullpen video, so I built the pipeline that gets it to them.

    Why the trainers, the onboarding guide, and the video pipeline exist

Pipeline
In use

Every bullpen, on video, with the pitch data next to it

I noticed that our pitchers didn't really have an easy way to watch their own bullpens. The clips sat inside Trackman's system without labels, and there was no link between a clip and the pitch it showed. So I built a job that pulls every Columbia bullpen clip through the Trackman API and pairs each one with that pitch's velocity, spin, movement, release, and location. It also renders a card beside the video, so a pitcher can see how that pitch compared with his own average for the pitch type.

The clips land in a shared Drive folder organized by pitcher, season, date, and pitch type, and every pitcher gets an email with a link when his new video is up. The whole team has access to it, and honestly the best part has been hearing that the pitchers actually use it.

12,419 bullpen clips · 25 pitchers · 149 sessions, Sep 2024 to Oct 2026 · Trackman API, Python, rclone, Google Apps Script

Pitch-Recognition Trainer

My freshman year I tagged pitches off a sticky note with four rules on it. I didn't really understand how velocity, spin, and movement fit together until a lot later. This game shows you those numbers from real Columbia pitches and asks you to call the pitch type, and it tells you right away whether you were right. The goal isn't to memorize one pitcher's arsenal, it's to build the mental model I didn't have when I started.

Trackman Tagging Trainer

When I started training new SABRLions on Trackman, I realized there really wasn't a low-stakes place to practice tagging before a live game, so I built one. The trainer walks through the whole sequence of reading the play, picking the pitch type, recording the result, and moving the runners. You can mess up and undo it without any real data at risk. For the staff
Weekly

The reports our pitching coach gets every week

Every week this fall I put together a one-page in-zone leaderboard for the pitching staff and a Monday development report for our pitching coach. The report tracks each pitcher against his own goals, with benchmarks from Division I, and after our scrimmages I write up each pitcher's outing. I also built a one-page D1 pitch-characteristics sheet so the staff can see how our arms compare with the rest of the country. Last spring I suggested using one of our pitchers as an opener after looking at inning-by-inning performance, and the coaches agreed it was a great idea.

I'm also helping build the SABRLions team website, with a profile for every player that holds his game and bullpen data, his bullpen video, and the weekly leaderboard. I really want players to be able to find their own information without having to ask for it, and we're working with Columbia's systems team to launch it.

Luck or skill? Your best hitter by OPS might just be getting lucky.

OPS tells you who produced, but not how or why, and some hitters are producing above what their batted balls say they should. I looked at every Division I team offense over four seasons to see what actually separates the good ones from the bad ones, and it was contact quality, not swing decisions and not swing-and-miss.

70%of the worst D1 offenses are bottom-third in contact quality
7%of the best offenses are
5xfaster: hard-hit% is reliable at about 61 PA, OPS at about 340

Bad and good offenses miss fastballs under 87 mph at the same rate, and the gap only opens as the velocity climbs. So the plan is weak contact gets bat speed and attack angle work, missing hard fastballs gets velocity training, and before you change anything you check it against past seasons and BP.

Research
Published

When can you trust a college stat?

Everyone says it's early in the season, but early could mean wait another weekend or wait until May, and I wanted to know which one. So I ran split-half reliability on the most common hitting and pitching stats, splitting each player's season in half at random and testing whether the same differences between players showed up in both halves. Whiff rate becomes dependable at about 59 plate appearances, and OPS still hasn't gotten there after a full season, since a typical D1 starter sees about 232. On the pitching side, velocity and movement are known after two fastballs, and runs allowed never reaches the threshold at all.

Research
In progress

Arm slot in college baseball

After reading a 2026 study that found sidearm pitchers in MLB carried less shoulder and elbow injury burden, I wanted to know whether any of that shows up in college. I'm using Division I Trackman data from 2022 through 2026 to estimate each pitcher's arm slot from where he releases the ball. The estimate is really stable, with the same pitcher landing in the same place from one half of a season to the next and mostly from one year to the next. Fewer than one in a hundred pitcher-seasons is a true submariner, and they sit in their own cluster on the plot. Across normal slots I haven't found a gradient in availability at all, while the submariners get used very differently, so the story so far is about the extreme tail and not a smooth slope. I won't call any gap in a pitcher's calendar an injury unless something outside the data confirms it.

D1, 2022–2026 · 17,180 pitcher-seasons with 100+ pitches · release geometry is a proxy for arm slot, not a measured arm angle

Research
In progress

What tracking data can, and cannot, say about mental performance

I wanted to see whether there was anything in the Trackman data that would show exactly when and where a pitcher started struggling, and then pair that with surveys and what we already know about a player's mental adversities or anxieties to see if there were connections there. On a small Columbia sample, release-point consistency looked almost perfectly reliable, and when I ran the same thing on about 1,900 Division I pitchers it fell to about 0.50, which is when the method had to change. The version I'm working on now measures whether a pitcher can repeat his release within a single outing, and I've been honest about the ideas that didn't hold up, especially anything that would try to rank players on something like composure from the data alone.

Two hitters, the same bat speed, completely different swings

Out of my Blast Motion work this fall. Rotational acceleration separates a swing the body turns from one the hands push, and because the barrel moves about three times faster than the hands, a small gap in hand speed becomes a big one at the barrel.

Story

Why Baseball, Player Development, Analytics, and Mental Performance Matter to Me

Some of my earliest meaningful baseball memories are going to Braves games at Turner Field with my dad. We were big Mets fans, but we lived in Knoxville, so Atlanta was the closest stadium to us. I loved going to the playground there, eating the famous hamburgers, and buying baseball cards from the Braves store with our Braves Bucks. Before one game, I got to go onto the field with the grounds crew and put in third base. I also watched my dad run onto the field with the Braves grounds crew, even though he was a Mets fan. I thought it was so cool because we got to go into the area where they stayed during games and see everything behind the scenes.

I loved listening to my dad talk about all the great players. I was obsessed with David Wright, Miguel Cabrera, Jacob deGrom, R.A. Dickey, Ted Williams, and basically everyone from the Baseball Card Adventures books by Dan Gutman. My dad had tons of baseball memorabilia in his office, and as a kid, I loved looking at it and thinking about its connections to the greats of baseball. On the drives home from Braves games to Knoxville, I would read the backs of baseball cards and tell my dad all about each player. I was obsessed with collecting and organizing my cards and looking through my dad’s old ones.

When I picture those childhood experiences, I remember feeling so happy and having a sense of belonging. I just loved being at baseball games because they were so fascinating to me. Baseball also connects me to my dad and to his father, whom I never met. My dad used to go to games with his father, just as I went to games with my dad. Knowing that we share those memories through baseball makes the game feel like part of my family.

I fell in love with baseball all over again in 2019, when I was fourteen. My dad told me about a Mets rookie named Pete Alonso who was supposed to be incredible, and I became fascinated with following the beginning of his major-league career and seeing how his performance and development continued. My favorite part of baseball is still seeing rookies make their MLB debuts. I love watching the beginning of a player’s career and seeing where his hard work takes him.

For a long time, I thought my future would revolve around math. I was obsessed with it as a kid and wanted to be like the women in Hidden Figures and work for NASA. Then I watched Moneyball and realized how exciting it would be to use math in baseball instead. When I went to college, I began working for the Columbia baseball team as a student manager. I loved being at the field and operating Trackman during games. The summer after my freshman year, I worked for the Oakland A’s in Baseball Operations and Analytics, which expanded my understanding of how information could be used within baseball.

I continued working for Columbia and learning as much as I could. By my junior year, I was working as many hours as possible and wanted to be at the field nonstop. It never felt like work. That was when I knew I was meant to do this. Working for the Dodgers before my senior year allowed me to continue learning and confirmed that baseball is where I am happiest and where I want to be.

Through those experiences, I realized that I am most interested in the combination of player development, analytics, and mental performance. To me, the three areas are closely connected. Analytics helps us understand what is happening in a player’s performance. Player development turns that information into a plan for improvement. Mental performance helps the player understand himself, respond to challenges, and consistently apply that plan.

Information matters most when a player can understand it, trust it, and use it. Two players can receive the same information and respond to it differently because they are different people with different experiences, goals, strengths, and ways of understanding the game. I want to be close to the daily development process: learning who players are, understanding what the information is showing, and communicating it in a way that is useful to each individual player. I want to understand what may be helping or preventing a player from making an improvement and work with him to develop an approach he can take ownership of.

Players interest me as people because they have a unique drive to succeed and a passion for the game. There are so many complexities to their lives and so many adversities they face, yet they continue pursuing their goals even when it gets incredibly hard. Many aspects of their lives influence how they view the game and determine their own success, and those are things other people may overlook.

What excites me most about player development is seeing a player’s happiness when his hard work begins to pay off. One of the ACL players I worked with improved drastically over the summer and is now in Low-A. He was one of the most hardworking and dedicated players I have ever known, and it was incredible to see him move up in the organization. Seeing his growth made me proud and reminded me why I want to do this work.

I find it especially meaningful to work with a player when he is struggling because it requires understanding what is happening and finding an approach that works for him. I also enjoy challenging a successful player because I get to see how he handles adversity and continues growing. In both situations, I want to help the player understand his development, evaluate himself, make adjustments, and take ownership of his career.

My interest in mental performance grew from noticing how players responded to failure, pressure, and things outside their control. Some players are incredibly hard on themselves. They take everything critically, are deeply affected by what people say to them, and can allow one mistake to change how they view themselves and their performance. I became interested in understanding those responses and helping players develop tools to work through them.

I have often felt most useful when I was listening to players, talking with them when they were struggling, and being someone they could speak to who was not a coach. Those experiences made me want to gain the knowledge and skills to help players better understand themselves, respond effectively to challenges, and make their own decisions. I see mental performance as part of the daily development process and something that can help every player get the most out of his abilities.

That work feels especially important in rookie ball. Players are beginning their professional careers while learning how to manage new expectations, responsibilities, routines, and constant evaluation. I want to be there at the beginning of that process and help players establish mental skills and self-awareness they can continue using throughout their careers. My fascination with the beginning of a player’s career is what brought me back to baseball in 2019, and it remains one of the parts of the game that excites me most.

I am also especially interested in working with international players. Every player brings an individual background, set of experiences, goals, strengths, and resources into professional baseball. Some international players are also navigating transitions involving language, culture, distance from family, or an unfamiliar professional system. Those experiences can influence development, communication, and mental performance in different ways.

International players bring their own knowledge, adaptability, perspectives, and ways of understanding the game. I want to take the time to learn about each player’s life, understand how he prefers to communicate, and recognize the strengths he already brings to his development.

For me, advocacy means listening to players and ensuring they have access to information and resources that are useful to them. It also means recognizing places where an organization can communicate more effectively or make its development systems more responsive to the people using them. I want to build trust, remain curious, and help each player understand his development, communicate what he needs, and take ownership of his career.

Ultimately, I want to bring together analytics, player development, and mental performance to help players get the most out of their abilities. Analytics allows me to understand performance, while relationships allow me to understand the person performing. I want my work to connect both.

I never believed it would be possible for me to work in baseball. Somehow, I am already living that dream, and it is still surreal to me. Baseball gives me happiness, a connection to my family, a sense of belonging, and a sense of purpose. Now I want to help players pursue their own goals.

That is what makes the exhausting or boring work worth doing. I love being at the field, talking with players and staff, learning about their lives, and doing whatever I can to support the development process. If I had to leave baseball, I would miss the people, the players, their dedication, and the feeling of seeing them improve. My life would feel empty, as though something important were missing. Baseball is where I belong, and helping players develop is the work I want to do.
