Why baseball

Some of my earliest meaningful baseball memories are going to Braves games at the old stadium, Turner Field, with my dad. We were big Mets fans, even though we did not go to Citi Field until I was nine or ten, maybe even older, but we lived in Knoxville and Atlanta was the closest stadium to us. I loved going to the playground there and eating the famous hamburgers. My dad would buy me baseball cards from the Braves store with our Braves Bucks. Before one Braves game, I got to go onto the field with the grounds crew and put in third base, and I watched my dad run onto the field with the Braves grounds crew even though he was a Mets fan. I thought it was so cool because we got to go into the area where they hung out during games and see everything.

I loved listening to my dad talk about all the great players. I was obsessed with David Wright, Miguel Cabrera, Jacob deGrom, R.A. Dickey, Ted Williams, and basically everyone from the Baseball Card Adventures books by Dan Gutman. My dad had tons of baseball memorabilia in his office, and as a kid I loved looking at it and thought it was so cool that he had so many things connected to the greats of baseball. On the drives home from Braves games to Knoxville, I would read the backs of baseball cards and tell my dad all about each player. I was obsessed with collecting and organizing baseball cards and looking through my dad's old cards, and I would show my elementary-school classmates who loved baseball the things my dad had and my Miguel Cabrera cards because I thought they were so cool. I remember going to a Smokies game on an elementary-school field trip and getting signatures from all the players, and I went to Tennessee baseball games with my dad and sister all the time.

When I picture those childhood experiences, I remember feeling so happy and having a sense of belonging. I just loved being at baseball games. They were so fascinating to me. Baseball gives me a connection to my dad and to his father, whom I never met. My dad used to go to games with his father, so he had those same kinds of memories with him. Baseball also gives me a sense of purpose. It makes me feel like I can help other people achieve their goals and live their dreams while I am already living mine.

Players interest me as people because they have a unique drive to succeed and a passion for the game. There are so many complexities to their lives and so many adversities they face, and even when it gets incredibly hard, they are still pursuing their dreams. There are many aspects of their lives that influence how they view the game and determine their own success, and those are complexities other people may overlook. Some players are so hard on themselves and take everything too critically, and that comes from how much they care about the game.

I especially care about helping struggling players because they have more at stake and more to lose. I want to help them work through adversity, continue growing, and have the opportunity to achieve their goals. I want to support the person behind the performance, including all the complexities and experiences that influence how they see themselves.

What excites me most about watching a player improve is seeing their excitement and happiness. I love seeing players achieve their goals, become better, and watching all their hard work pay off. One of the ACL players I worked with this summer improved drastically and is now in Low-A. He was one of the most hardworking and dedicated players I have ever known, and it was incredible to see him live his dream and move up in the organization.

I care most about helping a struggling player because they have more at stake and more to lose, and the payoff is even more rewarding when I see them succeed. I also like challenging a successful player because I get to see how they handle adversity and respond to a challenge, and I get to watch their continued growth as they work through it. I want to help players achieve their goals and feel that their hard work has paid off. I want to see them become better, grow, and continue pursuing their dreams no matter how hard it gets.

I found the game again in 2019, when I was fourteen, after my dad told me about a Mets rookie named Pete Alonso, and I became fascinated with following the beginning of a player's major-league career and watching how his performance and development continued. My favorite part of baseball is still seeing rookies make their MLB debuts. I love watching the beginning of a player's career and seeing where his hard work takes him.

Working with an ACL player who improved drastically and moved to Low-A showed me how meaningful it is to witness that early development. Young players care so much about the game, and they are pursuing their dreams no matter how difficult it gets. I want to be there while players grow, improve, respond to adversity, and begin achieving the goals they have worked so hard for. Helping players at the beginning of their careers connects directly to what first fascinated me about following rookies: seeing where their careers begin and how they continue to develop.

International players often have so much more at stake and so much more to lose than American players. Most American players have their families to go back to or have a college education, and many international players do not have those same luxuries. Baseball can mean so much more to international players because getting released or not improving carries many more risks, and they are pursuing their dreams despite the many complexities and adversities in their lives.

Advocating for international players means increasing awareness of what they have at stake. It also means trying to build more resources for them so they can have the same opportunities and fallbacks as American players. I want people to understand how much these players care about the game and how much they may be risking in the pursuit of their dreams. I want to help give them a real opportunity to grow, achieve their goals, and watch their hard work pay off.

I never believed working in baseball would be possible for me, and it is still surreal that I am already living my dream. I now want to help players achieve their goals and live their dreams too. I care about the players as people because of their dedication, complexity, and unique drive to succeed. I want to support them through adversity, watch them grow, and feel proud when their hard work pays off. If I had to leave baseball, I would miss everything. I would miss working with the people in baseball because everyone I have met so far has been incredible, I would miss the players and their dedication, and I would miss seeing players grow, achieve their goals, and feeling so proud of them. My life would feel empty and like something was missing.

Trackman Tagging Trainer

When I started training new SABRLions on Trackman, I realized there really wasn't a low-stakes place to practice tagging before a live game, so I built one. The trainer walks through the whole sequence of reading the play, picking the pitch type, recording the result, and moving the runners. You can mess up and undo it without any real data at risk. It's how our new taggers get their first reps now, and honestly I wish I'd had it my freshman year.

My freshman year I tagged pitches off a sticky note with four rules on it. I didn't really understand how velocity, spin, and movement fit together until a lot later. Research · in progress

What tracking data can, and cannot, say about mental performance

I wanted to study how pitchers respond to pressure and fatigue. The first thing I learned was that Trackman can measure what a delivery does, but it can't tell you what a player is thinking. On a small Columbia sample, release-point consistency looked almost perfectly reliable, and when I ran the same thing on about 1,900 Division I pitchers it fell to about 0.50. That was when the method had to change, and the version I'm working on now only measures whether a pitcher can repeat his release within a single outing. I've also been honest about the ideas that didn't hold up, especially anything that would rank players on something like composure.

Research · article in progress

When can you trust a college stat?

Everyone says it's early in the season, but early could mean wait another weekend or wait until May, and I wanted to know which one. So I ran split-half reliability on the most common hitting and pitching stats using Division I Trackman data from 2022 through 2026. The idea was to find out how many plate appearances or pitches each stat needs before it's real. Process stats like chase and whiff rate settle in around 30 plate appearances, and outcomes like walk rate take most of a season. What a pitch is takes about two pitches to know, while what a pitcher does with it takes a lot longer. The full write-up is coming on Medium.
